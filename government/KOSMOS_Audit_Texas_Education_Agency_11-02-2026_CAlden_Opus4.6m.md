# Executive Summary

The Texas Education Agency (TEA) is one of the largest educational governance systems in the United States, overseeing more than 5.4 million students across approximately 1,200 school districts and charter schools, with total K-12 expenditures exceeding $104 billion annually. This audit applies the KOSMOS 7ES/FDP/DQD/OCF analytical framework to evaluate whether the TEA system optimizes for mutual benefit across all participants or whether it functions as an extraction architecture that concentrates benefits among privileged subpopulations while distributing costs to vulnerable communities.

**Principal Findings:**

The TEA system exhibits a **dominant extractive pattern** across multiple dimensions. The system's operational architecture reveals:

1. **Centralized authority without democratic accountability.** The Commissioner of Education — an unelected appointee of the Governor — wields statutory power to replace democratically elected school boards and superintendents. This power has been exercised against the largest and second-largest school districts in the state (Houston ISD, Fort Worth ISD), both serving predominantly Black and Hispanic student populations.

2. **Systematic denial of services to vulnerable populations.** A federal investigation confirmed that TEA illegally capped special education enrollment at 8.5%, denying tens of thousands of students with disabilities their federally mandated services. TEA denied the cap existed even as its own monitoring system incentivized districts to deny services.

3. **Ideological capture of curriculum standards.** The State Board of Education has repeatedly rejected science textbooks that present scientific consensus on evolution and climate change, appointing textbook reviewers with anti-evolution and climate-denial credentials who pressured publishers to alter content. This pattern subordinates evidence-based education to ideological commitments.

4. **Dark money influence on policy direction.** Organizations such as the Texas Public Policy Foundation (TPPF) — funded by oil billionaires Tim Dunn and Farris Wilks, and connected to ALEC — have driven the voucher agenda that now diverts $1 billion in public funds to private schools, predominantly benefiting families already enrolled in private education.

5. **Accountability mechanisms weaponized against local control.** The A-F accountability system, built primarily on STAAR test performance, has been challenged in court for AI grading errors, retroactive rating methodology changes, and psychometric deficiencies — yet functions as the primary mechanism through which the Commissioner justifies state takeovers of local districts.

# Phase 1: Structural Dissection — 7ES Analysis

## Element 1: Input

### 1.1 Financial Inputs

The TEA receives inputs from three primary funding streams:

- **State Foundation School Program (FSP):** Approximately $50+ billion biennially, distributed via per-pupil allotments with weighted adjustments for special education, bilingual education, career and technical education, and compensatory education.
- **Federal Funds:** $3.9 billion in FY2024, including $1.87 billion in Title I funds for low-income students and $1.2 billion in IDEA funds for special education.
- **Permanent School Fund (PSF):** A $44+ billion endowment — the largest educational endowment in the nation — managed in part by TEA staff and the SBOE, distributing approximately $2.5 billion biennially to schools.

**Subsystem Analysis:** The input architecture creates structural dependency on state legislative appropriations. The Legislature has not adjusted the Basic Allotment for inflation since 2019 (raised from $6,160 to $6,215 per student in HB 2 in 2025 — a 0.9% increase against cumulative inflation exceeding 20%). The PSF, despite its massive size, has been criticized for underperforming and distributing less per student than it did decades ago, while accumulating higher management fees and reduced transparency.

### 1.2 Regulatory Inputs

- **Texas Education Code:** Statutory authority governing TEA operations, including §39A.111 granting the Commissioner power to appoint boards of managers replacing elected trustees.
- **Federal Law:** IDEA, ESEA/ESSA, Title IX, and other federal mandates.
- **SBOE Curriculum Standards (TEKS):** Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills standards set by the 15-member elected board.

### 1.3 Human Capital Inputs

- Over 370,000 teachers statewide, with persistent teacher shortages, particularly in rural and high-poverty districts.
- An agency workforce at TEA headquarters that a 2026 state audit found had a management-heavy structure exceeding statutory limitations on management-to-staff ratios.

**Counterfactual (Nader lens):** If input streams were designed for equitable student benefit rather than political control, the Basic Allotment would be indexed to inflation, the PSF would maximize distributions rather than asset accumulation, and federal special education funds would flow without the artificial barriers that TEA historically imposed.

## Element 2: Output

### 2.1 Primary Outputs

- **Funding Distribution:** TEA describes itself as "largely a pass-through vehicle to fund schools." Total K-12 expenditures reached $104.9 billion in 2024-25, or $18,972 per pupil.
- **Accountability Ratings:** A-F letter grades assigned to every school and district, based primarily on STAAR test performance, graduation rates, and College/Career/Military Readiness (CCMR) indicators.
- **Curriculum Standards:** TEKS documents guiding instruction across all subjects.
- **Voucher Administration:** The Texas Education Freedom Accounts (TEFA) program, distributing approximately $10,474 per standard student and up to $30,000 per special education student to private schools.
- **Regulatory Compliance Determinations:** Including charter school authorization, special education monitoring, and district intervention/takeover decisions.

### 2.2 Output Distribution Analysis (Dominant Pattern Priority Rule)

**Who benefits from outputs?**

- **Voucher program:** Early data shows 70% of private school voucher recipients were already enrolled in private school. Out of 5.45 million public school students, only approximately 68,000 even applied — barely 1%. The more economically disadvantaged a district, the fewer families applied. The program is projected to cost public schools $300 million in lost state funding while potentially swelling to $4.4 billion next biennium. This is a subsidy to families who can already afford private education, not an access mechanism for the underserved.

- **Accountability outputs:** The A-F system has been challenged in multiple lawsuits. Courts have temporarily blocked ratings releases. AI grading of STAAR writing responses was implemented without adequate psychometric validation. Retroactive methodology changes altered ratings after students had already graduated. Districts receiving low ratings become vulnerable to state takeover — a mechanism that concentrates power in the Commissioner's office.

- **Achievement outcomes:** NAEP scores show Texas eighth-graders performing below national averages in reading and math. Persistent achievement gaps remain between white students and Black and Hispanic peers — gaps that have remained essentially unchanged since the 1990s. Even at "A" and "B" rated schools, students of color dramatically underperform their white peers.

**Counterfactual (Baldwin lens):** If the system's outputs served all children equally, the $1 billion diverted to vouchers would instead close the funding gaps that keep Black, Hispanic, and rural students behind. The 8.5% special education cap would never have existed because the system would have been designed to find and serve every child with a disability, not to minimize costs by denying services.

## Element 3: Processing

### 3.1 Decision-Making Architecture

The TEA processing architecture concentrates critical decisions in three nodes:

1. **The Commissioner of Education (Appointed):** Mike Morath, appointed by Governor Abbott in 2016, is not an educator — he is a software developer and investor who served on the Dallas ISD board. He wields unilateral power to: replace elected school boards with appointed managers; impose sanctions on districts; direct curriculum implementation; fine testing contractors; and extend state takeovers indefinitely (HISD takeover extended to 2027 despite reported academic improvements).

2. **The State Board of Education (Elected, 15 members):** Sets curriculum standards and oversees the Permanent School Fund. However, real operational authority has migrated from this elected body to the appointed Commissioner through successive legislative actions. The Governor appoints the SBOE Chair from among the elected members, further concentrating executive influence.

3. **The Texas Legislature:** Sets funding levels, creates programs (including the voucher program), and has progressively expanded Commissioner authority while narrowing SBOE powers.

### 3.2 Processing Failures

- **STAAR Testing:** The testing system has been called a "perversion of its original intent" by a former TEA Commissioner (Robert Scott). STAAR critics include 50+ superintendents who formally complained to TEA, parents who identify excessive stress and lost instructional time, and attorneys who have successfully obtained injunctions blocking accountability ratings.
- **AI Grading:** TEA implemented AI grading of STAAR written responses without the psychometric validation standards typically required for high-stakes assessments.
- **Special Education Processing:** The 8.5% enrollment cap created a processing system where districts were incentivized to deny evaluations to students suspected of having disabilities — using "Response to Intervention" approaches as gatekeeping mechanisms rather than identification tools.

**Counterfactual (Scott lens):** James C. Scott's concept of "legibility" is inverted here. Rather than the state making local systems legible to enable beneficial governance, TEA uses standardized testing to create a legibility framework that enables punitive control — the A-F system doesn't illuminate educational quality so much as it creates the bureaucratic justification for centralized takeover of locally governed institutions.

## Element 4: Controls

### 4.1 Formal Controls

- **Texas Education Code:** Statutory framework governing all TEA operations.
- **Federal Compliance Requirements:** IDEA, ESSA, civil rights laws.
- **State Auditor Oversight:** The 2026 State Auditor's report found TEA's hiring practices created a management-heavy structure violating statutory limitations — indicating that even formal controls fail to constrain the agency.
- **Sunset Review:** TEA is subject to Sunset review, with the agency scheduled for potential abolition on September 1, 2029 unless reauthorized.

### 4.2 Shadow Controls

- **Governor's Office:** The Governor appoints the Commissioner, appoints the SBOE Chair, and has used primary election campaigns to remove legislators who opposed the voucher agenda. Governor Abbott led a 2024 campaign to oust Republican lawmakers who opposed vouchers, making education policy subordinate to executive political priorities.
- **Texas Public Policy Foundation (TPPF):** Founded in 1989 with a primary aim of privatizing public education through voucher subsidies. TPPF is a member and founder of ALEC. Its vice chairman, Tim Dunn, is a West Texas oil billionaire. TPPF's fundraising letter prior to the 88th legislature stated: "Public education is GROUND ZERO in the fight for freedom. The policy team and board of TPPF believe it is now or never."
- **Dark Money Networks:** Common Cause Texas identified networks through which billionaires including Tim Dunn, Farris Wilks, and the Walton family funnel money through intermediary organizations to influence education policy, SBOE elections, and legislative races.

**Counterfactual (Herman/Chomsky lens):** The Manufacturing Consent framework is directly applicable. The formal control structure (elected SBOE, public comment periods, open meetings) creates the appearance of democratic governance. The operational control structure (Governor appointments, dark money influence, TPPF-ALEC policy pipeline, Commissioner's unilateral takeover authority) reveals the actual decision-making architecture — one where oil billionaires and privatization advocates exercise more influence over Texas education than the parents and communities whose children attend the schools.

## Element 5: Feedback

### 5.1 Active Feedback Mechanisms

- **STAAR Testing/A-F Ratings:** The primary formal feedback loop. However, this mechanism is deeply contested — challenged in multiple lawsuits, blocked by judicial injunctions, criticized for AI grading without validation, and acknowledged by a former Commissioner as a "perversion."
- **Public Comment Periods:** SBOE holds public hearings on curriculum standards and textbook adoptions. However, testimony from thousands of scientists and education experts has been repeatedly overridden by board members with ideological commitments to creationism and climate denial. Public comment exists as process, not as functional feedback.
- **Federal Monitoring:** The U.S. Department of Education's special education investigation provided external feedback that TEA had violated federal law. TEA's initial response was denial — claiming the 8.5% cap was an "indicator," not a cap, despite evidence that districts treated it exactly as a cap.

### 5.2 Feedback Failures

- **Parent/Community Feedback on Takeovers:** Houston community members, elected officials, and the mayor all opposed the HISD takeover. The takeover proceeded without community engagement or transparency, according to the mayor's statement. The feedback mechanism — elections, public comment, community organizing — proved entirely non-functional against the Commissioner's statutory authority.
- **Teacher Feedback:** Under HISD's state-appointed Superintendent Mike Miles, the district experienced extraordinary staff turnover, library closures (converted to "team centers"), and a strict curriculum emphasizing test scores. Teacher opposition was not incorporated as corrective feedback but treated as resistance to be overcome.
- **Legislative Feedback:** HB 2657 was filed to abolish TEA entirely — an extreme feedback signal from within the Legislature itself that the agency has lost institutional legitimacy. The bill proposed transferring TEA functions to the SBOE and Comptroller.

**Passive Feedback (System Persistence):** TEA continues to exist primarily because of legislative reauthorization cycles and the recursive belief of participants that the system serves educational purposes. The Sunset review date of September 1, 2029 provides a constitutional mechanism for system dissolution — but the same political forces that control TEA's operations also control the Sunset process.

**Counterfactual (Sagan lens):** Carl Sagan's epistemological standard demands that claims be proportional to evidence. TEA claims to "improve outcomes for all public school students." The evidence shows: NAEP scores below national averages, achievement gaps unchanged since the 1990s, tens of thousands of students with disabilities illegally denied services, $1 billion diverted to families already in private schools, and elected governance replaced with appointed control in majority-minority districts. The claim of universal benefit is unsupported by the available evidence.

## Element 6: Interface

### 6.1 External Interfaces

- **Federal Government:** TEA receives and administers federal funds, submits compliance reports, and has been subject to federal investigation and corrective action orders.
- **School Districts (1,200+):** TEA's primary interface with the local level. This interface is primarily regulatory and punitive — accountability ratings, compliance monitoring, intervention, and takeover.
- **Private Schools (via TEFA):** The voucher program creates a new interface between the state and private schools, but with dramatically reduced accountability requirements compared to public schools. Private schools receiving vouchers are not required to administer STAAR tests, meet the same teacher certification requirements, or comply with the same special education mandates.
- **Testing Contractors:** TEA contracts with companies like ETS for STAAR administration. A scoring error by ETS resulted in a $20.7 million fine — yet the same flawed testing architecture continues to drive high-stakes accountability decisions.

### 6.2 Interface Asymmetry

The interface between TEA and school districts is fundamentally asymmetric. TEA possesses regulatory, financial, and administrative power over districts; districts have no reciprocal mechanism to hold TEA accountable. When districts challenged A-F ratings in court, TEA's response characterized them as "a small group of school boards and superintendents opposed to fair accountability and transparency" — reframing institutional accountability challenges as anti-transparency.

The interface between TEA and private schools (via vouchers) exhibits the opposite asymmetry — minimal regulatory oversight, no standardized testing requirements, no A-F ratings, and no takeover authority. The system applies maximum accountability pressure to public schools while exempting private schools receiving public funds from equivalent scrutiny.

**Counterfactual (Nader lens):** If the interface were designed for consumer (student/family) protection rather than institutional control, the accountability framework would apply equally to all schools receiving public funds. Private schools accepting vouchers would be subject to the same testing, reporting, and takeover provisions as public schools. The information asymmetry between TEA and parents — where TEA possesses all data and parents must navigate opaque bureaucratic systems — would be eliminated through mandatory proactive disclosure.

## Element 7: Environment

### 7.1 Political Environment

- **One-Party Governance:** Texas has been governed by the Republican Party at all statewide levels for over two decades. This eliminates the inter-party competition that might otherwise constrain executive overreach in education policy.
- **Gerrymandered SBOE Districts:** The 15 SBOE districts were drawn through a process controlled by the same party that appoints the Commissioner, creating structural alignment between the elected board and the executive appointee.
- **Primary Election as Control Mechanism:** Governor Abbott's 2024 campaign to oust anti-voucher Republicans in primaries demonstrates that the political environment punishes legislators who resist the privatization agenda, regardless of constituent preferences.

### 7.2 Economic Environment

- Texas has no state income tax, creating structural dependence on property taxes and sales taxes for education funding — regressive revenue sources that distribute costs disproportionately to lower-income populations.
- The oil and gas industry — the source of wealth for TPPF's major funders — benefits from a population educated to be skeptical of climate science and favorable to fossil fuel extraction.
- The $104 billion K-12 education system represents a massive market opportunity for private education operators, testing companies, curriculum vendors, and charter management organizations.

### 7.3 Demographic Environment

- 53% Hispanic, 26% White, 13% African American student population.
- 61% economically disadvantaged.
- 22% emergent bilingual.
- Texas educates the largest number of rural children in the United States.
- The populations most affected by TEA's extractive patterns — Black, Hispanic, rural, low-income, and special education students — are also the populations with the least political power to resist those patterns.

# Phase 2: Ethical Benchmarking — FDP Scoring

**Domain Classification: Governance**
**Weight Vector: DA (3), RE (2), AR (2); all others weight 1**

## FDP 1: Symbiotic Purpose (SP) — Score: 1.5/10

**Formula:** SP = 10 × (Benefits to all stakeholders / Benefits to controllers)

**Analysis:** The TEA system claims to "improve outcomes for all public school students." Documented evidence reveals:

- **Achievement gaps unchanged since the 1990s** between white students and students of color, even at top-rated schools.
- **NAEP scores below national averages** in 8th grade reading and math.
- **Tens of thousands of students with disabilities illegally denied services** through the 8.5% cap.
- **$1 billion voucher program** where 70% of recipients were already in private school, projected to cost public schools $300 million in lost funding.
- **State takeovers of majority-minority districts** (Houston ISD, Fort Worth ISD) replacing elected governance with appointed management.
- **Curriculum decisions driven by ideology** rather than scientific consensus.

The system's outputs primarily benefit: (a) the political apparatus that controls TEA (Governor, aligned legislators); (b) private education providers receiving public funds with minimal accountability; (c) affluent families receiving voucher subsidies for private education they were already purchasing; and (d) ideological interests that shape curriculum to reflect religious and political preferences rather than scientific evidence.

The 5.4 million students — particularly the 61% who are economically disadvantaged, the 53% who are Hispanic, and the 13% who are African American — bear the costs of underfunding, denial of services, culturally hostile curriculum, and loss of democratic local governance.

**Dominant Pattern Priority Rule applied:** The system imposes costs on the majority of affected participants (5.4 million students, particularly the most vulnerable) while concentrating benefits among a minority (political controllers, private education operators, affluent voucher recipients). The extractive pattern characterizes greater than 60% of system operations.

**Score Justification:** SP = 1.5. Minimal credit for the existence of the funding pass-through mechanism, which does distribute resources to schools. Zero credit for the claimed purpose of "improving outcomes for all" given documented failure to close achievement gaps, illegal denial of special education, and systematic diversion of funds to private interests.

## FDP 2: Adaptive Resilience (AR) — Score: 2.0/10

**Formula:** AR = 10 × (1 − External interventions / Autonomous processes)

**Analysis:**

- **Federal intervention required** to correct illegal special education cap — TEA did not self-correct; it denied the cap existed until forced by federal investigation.
- **Judicial intervention required** to block flawed A-F accountability ratings — courts had to intervene to prevent release of ratings based on contested methodology.
- **Legislative intervention proposed** (HB 2657) to abolish TEA entirely — the most extreme form of external intervention signal.
- **Sunset review mechanism** required for continued agency existence.
- **No autonomous correction of voucher inequity** — early data showing 70% of recipients already in private school has not prompted program redesign.
- **STAAR testing system** described as a "perversion of its original intent" by a former Commissioner, yet persisted for over a decade before legislative overhaul.
- **State takeover of HISD extended** despite reported academic improvement — the system cannot self-correct toward returning local control.

**Score Justification:** AR = 2.0. The system exhibits minimal capacity for self-correction. Error correction occurs only through external intervention (federal investigation, lawsuits, legislative action). When internal feedback signals exist (community opposition, expert testimony, achievement data), they are systematically overridden.

## FDP 3: Reciprocal Ethics (RE) — Score: 1.0/10

**Formula:** RE = 10 × (Fair exchanges / Total exchanges)

**Analysis:**

- **Funding exchange:** Students and communities contribute (via property taxes, sales taxes) and receive educational services — but the exchange is systematically unfair. The Basic Allotment has not kept pace with inflation. Rural and low-income communities receive less effective educational investment. The voucher program extracts $300 million from public schools to subsidize families who can already afford private education.

- **Labor exchange:** Teachers provide instruction but face persistent shortages, inadequate compensation, and punitive accountability systems. Under state takeover in HISD, hundreds of employees were terminated or reassigned, libraries were converted to "team centers," and staff turnover was described as "extraordinary."

- **Governance exchange:** Communities elect school boards, but those boards can be replaced by the Commissioner's appointed managers — nullifying the democratic contract. Taxpayers fund the system but cannot meaningfully influence how their money is spent when dark money organizations outweigh their political voice.

- **Special education exchange:** Families of children with disabilities pay taxes supporting the system; the system illegally denied their children services for over a decade.

- **Accountability exchange:** Public schools are subject to high-stakes testing, A-F ratings, and state takeover. Private schools receiving the same public funds are exempt from equivalent accountability.

**Score Justification:** RE = 1.0. Virtually no exchanges within the system qualify as fair or reciprocal. Costs are systematically distributed to vulnerable populations (low-income, minority, rural, special education), while benefits flow to politically connected interests (Governor's office, privatization advocates, affluent families, private school operators).

## FDP 4: Closed-Loop Materiality (CLM) — Score: 1.5/10

**Formula:** CLM = 10 × (Recycled outputs / Total outputs)

**Analysis:**

- **Financial waste:** The Permanent School Fund has been criticized for underperformance and high management fees — a Houston Chronicle investigation found the fund "should be billions larger than it is." Resources leak out of the educational loop through management fees, administrative bloat (documented in the 2026 state audit), and diversion to private schools via vouchers.

- **Human capital waste:** Teacher shortages persist while the system drives teachers out through punitive accountability, inadequate compensation, and the demoralization of state takeovers. Trained teachers leaving the profession represent unrecycled system outputs.

- **Student outcome waste:** Students denied special education services become adults with unaddressed disabilities — a permanent loss to both individuals and the broader society. Students receiving ideology-driven science education rather than evidence-based instruction carry knowledge deficits into higher education and the workforce.

- **Democratic governance waste:** When elected school boards are replaced by appointed managers, the civic capacity built through local democratic governance is destroyed. Communities lose institutional knowledge, democratic practice, and the social capital of self-governance.

- **Voucher leakage:** $1 billion flows from public education to private institutions with no recycling mechanism — no accountability data flows back, no special education services are guaranteed, and no failed private schools face the same consequences as public schools.

**Score Justification:** CLM = 1.5. The system exhibits massive waste across financial, human capital, democratic, and educational dimensions. Resources flow out of the educational commons into private hands with minimal recycling.

## FDP 5: Distributed Agency (DA) — Score: 1.0/10

**Formula:** DA = 10 × (1 − Centralized decisions / Total decisions)

**Analysis:**

This is the TEA system's most critical failure. Decision-making power is concentrated in a three-node hierarchy:

1. **Governor** → Appoints Commissioner, appoints SBOE Chair, campaigns to elect compliant legislators, signs voucher legislation.
2. **Commissioner** → Unelected. Wields unilateral power to replace elected school boards, impose accountability sanctions, direct curriculum implementation, extend takeovers indefinitely.
3. **SBOE** → Nominally elected but subject to dark money influence in elections, with the Chair appointed by the Governor. Legislative actions have progressively transferred SBOE authority to the Commissioner.

**Evidence of centralization:**
- Commissioner replaced elected boards in Houston ISD (200,000 students) and Fort Worth ISD — the two largest takeovers in state history.
- Governor used primary election campaigns to punish legislators who opposed his education agenda.
- Dark money from a small number of billionaires (Dunn, Wilks, Walton) flows through opaque organizations to influence SBOE elections and legislative races.
- The Commissioner's decision to extend the HISD takeover to 2027 was made unilaterally, despite reported academic improvements that would seem to justify restoring local control.

**Constraint Type 9 Override (Democratic Legitimacy Framing):** The SBOE is elected, but this does not constitute distributed agency when: (a) elections are influenced by dark money from a handful of billionaires; (b) the Governor appoints the Chair; (c) real authority has migrated from the elected SBOE to the appointed Commissioner through legislative action; and (d) the Governor campaigns to remove legislators who resist his education agenda. The electoral mechanism exists, but it does not produce accountability to the governed population.

**Score Justification:** DA = 1.0. Decision-making is concentrated in an appointed Commissioner and a Governor who controls the Commissioner's appointment, the SBOE Chair's appointment, and the legislative landscape through primary election campaigns. Local communities have no effective mechanism to resist centralized control over their schools.

## FDP 6: Contextual Harmony (CH) — Score: 2.0/10

**Formula:** CH = 10 × (Positive local impacts / Total impacts)

**Analysis:**

- **Rural impact:** Texas educates the largest number of rural children in the U.S. The voucher program offers minimal benefit to rural families, where private school options are scarce or nonexistent. Yet rural schools lose state funding when students leave, regardless of whether alternatives exist.

- **Cultural impact:** A 53% Hispanic student population is served by a curriculum development process historically dominated by ideological debates about U.S. history, evolution, and climate change, with minimal responsiveness to cultural and linguistic diversity.

- **Community impact:** State takeovers destroy local educational governance. Houston's community — elected officials, teachers' unions, parents, civil rights organizations — universally opposed the takeover. The Commissioner proceeded regardless.

- **Economic impact:** Public schools serve as economic anchors in many Texas communities, providing employment, social services, and community gathering spaces. Voucher programs that drain enrollment also drain the economic vitality of communities dependent on their public schools.

- **Ecological impact:** SBOE rejection of science textbooks presenting climate change consensus actively harms the local ecological context by producing graduates who are skeptical of the science needed to address environmental challenges facing Texas (droughts, flooding, coastal erosion, extreme heat).

**Score Justification:** CH = 2.0. The system disrupts local communities through takeovers, drains local resources through vouchers, ignores cultural context in curriculum, and actively undermines environmental literacy. Some credit for the sheer existence of public schools as community institutions — but the system's direction of travel is toward dismantling those institutions.

## FDP 7: Emergent Transparency (ET) — Score: 0.5/10

- **Special education cap:** TEA denied the 8.5% cap existed — claiming it was an "indicator" — while its own monitoring system incentivized districts to deny services. This is active concealment of a policy that violated federal law.
- **A-F methodology:** Retroactive changes to accountability methodology were applied after students had already graduated. AI grading of STAAR writing was implemented without disclosure of validation studies. Lawsuits seeking psychometric analysis of STAAR were met with resistance.
- **PSF management:** The Permanent School Fund shifted toward riskier assets with "limited transparency and higher management fees" — per Houston Chronicle investigation.
- **Dark money:** The influence networks funding SBOE elections and education policy operate through 501(c)(4) organizations that do not disclose donors — making the actual decision-making architecture opaque to the public.
- **HISD takeover:** Houston Mayor Sylvester Turner stated the process was "without community engagement or any transparency."
- **Voucher data:** Early data on voucher distribution required public information requests from advocacy organizations — not proactive disclosure by the state.
- **TEA non-responsiveness:** Texas Monthly reported that a researcher "tried to get information from the Texas Education Agency that might explain the low [STAAR] scores, but she got no response."

**Estimated Verifiable Processes:** ~15%
**Estimated Withheld Data:** ~60%

**ET Calculation:** (10 × 0.15) − (2 × 60) = 1.5 − 1.2 = 0.3, rounded to **0.5** (floor for having some public financial data available).

**Score Justification:** ET = 0.5. The system operates with systematic opacity across its most consequential processes: special education policy, testing methodology, investment management, political influence networks, and takeover decision-making.

**Score Justification:** IH = 1.0. The system consistently misrepresents its operations, conceals trade-offs, denies documented harms, and frames extraction as empowerment. Almost every major TEA policy position involves a significant gap between the stated rationale and the documented reality.

# Counterfactuals

## Counterfactual 1: What if TEA had not implemented the 8.5% special education cap?

If TEA had identified and served all students with disabilities at the national average rate of 13% (rather than artificially capping at 8.5%), approximately 250,000 additional students would have received special education services over the cap's 13-year duration (2004-2017). These students would have received IEPs, specialized instruction, and related services mandated by federal law. The long-term cost savings from early intervention would likely have exceeded the service provision costs, as unserved disabilities compound over time into more expensive adult social service needs. This counterfactual alone demonstrates the system's extractive optimization: it was cheaper to deny services than to provide them, and the system chose extraction over the wellbeing of children with disabilities.

## Counterfactual 2: What if the $1 billion voucher budget had been invested in public schools?

If the $1 billion allocated to TEFA had instead been distributed to public schools through the Foundation School Program, it would have increased the Basic Allotment by approximately $185 per student — or targeted to high-need districts, it could have funded intensive literacy and math intervention programs in the schools serving the 61% of economically disadvantaged students. Given that the voucher program primarily subsidizes families already in private school (70% of recipients), the alternative investment would have served a population approximately 80 times larger (5.4 million vs. ~68,000 applicants) with higher need.

## Counterfactual 3: What if curriculum decisions were made by subject-matter experts rather than ideologically driven SBOE members?

If textbook review panels were composed of scientists, educators, and subject-matter experts — rather than including anti-evolution activists and climate-denial advocates appointed by SBOE members — Texas students would receive science education aligned with scientific consensus. Given that Texas is the second-largest textbook market in the country, this change would ripple nationally, improving science literacy across multiple states.

## Counterfactual 4: What if the Commissioner were elected rather than appointed?

If the Commissioner of Education were directly elected by Texas voters rather than appointed by the Governor, the Commissioner would be accountable to the public rather than to the executive political agenda. The Commissioner's power to replace elected school boards would create a democratic paradox — an elected official overriding other elected officials — that might constrain takeover authority in practice. An elected Commissioner would also be insulated from the Governor's use of the position to advance voucher and privatization agendas.

## Counterfactual 5: What if dark money were prohibited in SBOE and education-related legislative races?

If Texas education policy were insulated from undisclosed political spending, the influence of organizations like TPPF, Empower Texans, and dark money PACs funded by oil billionaires would be dramatically reduced. SBOE elections would be decided by voters rather than by campaign spending from undisclosed sources. Curriculum decisions would more likely reflect community values and expert consensus rather than the ideological preferences of a small number of wealthy donors.
