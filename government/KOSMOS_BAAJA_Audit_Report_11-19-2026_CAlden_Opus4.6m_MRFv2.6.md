# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (BAAJA, S. 5653) is a sweeping federal permitting reform bill introduced on September 30, 2026, by Senators Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV), Mike Lee (R-UT), Martin Heinrich (D-NM), and Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI). The bill proposes over 75 amendments to bedrock environmental laws — NEPA, the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, and the National Historic Preservation Act — while also reforming electricity transmission planning and requiring data centers to pay their grid infrastructure costs.

This audit applies the KOSMOS 7ES/FDP/DQD/OCF framework to evaluate BAAJA as a legislative system. The analysis reveals a system whose dominant pattern optimizes for corporate energy infrastructure acceleration while systematically weakening the protective mechanisms designed to ensure communities, tribal nations, ecosystems, and democratic feedback loops can constrain extraction.

**Key Findings:**

| Metric | Score | Classification |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| Global FDP (Weighted, Governance) | **1.42** | Unnatural (Collapse-Prone) |
| DQD | **0.65** | Unnatural |
| OCF | **0.31** | Hybrid (Moderate Risk) |
| Constraint Cascade (CSI) | **0.31** | Severe Cascade |

The bill's architecture exhibits a characteristic extraction pattern: benefits flow upward to energy corporations (fossil and clean alike), tech companies, and project developers, while costs — in the form of weakened environmental review, reduced tribal sovereignty, restricted judicial remedies, and locked-in fossil infrastructure — flow downward to communities, indigenous nations, ecosystems, and future generations.

The "bipartisan" label and "affordability" framing serve as legitimation architecture. The American Petroleum Institute — the lobbying arm of major oil and gas corporations — has identified permitting reform as its top legislative priority, and over 100 trade groups have endorsed the bill. Meanwhile, nine tribal organizations have formally opposed it, the Sierra Club, NRDC, Earthjustice, and the Southern Environmental Law Center oppose it, and approximately 100 environmental and energy law scholars have warned that BAAJA "would inflict permanent, sweeping damage on the nation's core environmental laws."

The modest projected household savings of $50–78 per year by 2040 (Rhodium Group estimate) must be weighed against the permanent structural weakening of environmental protections that have safeguarded public health, waterways, endangered species, and tribal cultural resources for over fifty years.

# 1. PHASE 1: STRUCTURAL DISSECTION — 7ES SYSTEM ANATOMY

## 1.1 Input

BAAJA's input architecture reveals the power dynamics shaping the legislation:

**Primary Inputs:**
- **Legislative Text:** S. 5653, introduced September 30, 2026, containing two divisions and seven titles spanning permitting reform, transmission planning, and energy infrastructure
- **Industry Lobbying (Dominant Input):** Over 100 trade groups formally endorsed the bill, including the American Petroleum Institute, National Mining Association, American Gas Association, Independent Petroleum Association of America, Liquid Energy Pipeline Association, and the Institute of Makers of Explosives. The fossil fuel industry's lobbying apparatus provided Republican staff with full bill-drafting capacity, while Democratic senatorial offices negotiated with comparatively limited analytical support
- **Tech Industry Input:** Data center operators and AI companies lobbied for provisions enabling faster grid connections and infrastructure buildout, with groups like the American Conservation Coalition spending $300,000 lobbying for BAAJA
- **Clean Energy Industry Input:** American Clean Power Association supported the bill's transmission and permitting provisions
- **Labor Input:** AFL-CIO and International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers (IBEW) endorsed the bill
- **Existing Environmental Law:** NEPA (1970), Clean Water Act (1972), Endangered Species Act (1973), National Historic Preservation Act (1966) — all targeted for amendment
- **Executive Input:** President Trump endorsed BAAJA on Truth Social, calling it "a BIG WIN for U.S. ENERGY INDEPENDENCE and DOMINANCE"

**Marginalized Inputs:**
- **Tribal Nations:** Nine tribal organizations wrote Congress in opposition; Standing Rock Sioux, California tribal leaders, and the Native American Rights Fund raised formal objections — but the bill's drafting did not incorporate meaningful tribal consultation
- **Environmental Organizations:** Sierra Club, NRDC, Earthjustice, Center for Biological Diversity, Southern Environmental Law Center — all opposed, but their input was not reflected in the bill's provisions
- **Environmental Justice Communities:** UU Service Justice coalition, Climate Justice Alliance, and others representing BIPOC communities raised objections about disproportionate impacts
- **Academic Experts:** ~100 environmental and energy law scholars warned of "permanent, sweeping damage" — their letter arrived days before cloture

**7ES Assessment:** Input is heavily privilege-calibrated. Industry interests had asymmetric access to the drafting process — Republican staff had the "full lobbying, analyzing, and bill-drafting capacity of the entire fossil fuel industry," while Democratic staff negotiated with limited support. Tribal, environmental justice, and community inputs were structurally marginalized despite formal opposition.

## 1.2 Output

**Primary Outputs:**
- Streamlined environmental review with reduced NEPA scope, narrowed alternatives analysis, expanded exemptions, and shortened timelines
- Weakened Endangered Species Act protections, including potential state delegation that "may make certain species more vulnerable to political hostility"
- Weakened Clean Water Act: state certification authority narrowed, NPDES permit terms doubled (5→10 years), "clear and convincing evidence" standard imposed for certification denials
- Weakened National Historic Preservation Act: tribal consultation rights reduced, Section 106 protections undermined
- Project Certainty (Title IV): agencies prohibited from revoking/altering permits; courts cannot vacate NEPA-deficient permits (remand without vacatur only); project sponsors can sue for 25–50% of total project costs
- FERC backstop siting authority for interstate transmission
- Data center cost allocation requirements (20+ MW facilities pay incremental grid costs)
- Accelerated permitting for both fossil fuel AND clean energy projects
- Reduced standing to sue (participation in comment process required)
- Standardized public comment periods (45–75 days)

**Output Distribution Analysis:**
- **Benefits to controllers/industry:** Permit certainty, reduced regulatory burden, faster project timelines, reduced litigation risk, locked-in infrastructure
- **Benefits to general public:** Modest electricity cost reduction ($50–78/yr by 2040), data center cost protection, potential clean energy acceleration
- **Costs to affected communities:** Weakened environmental protections, reduced tribal sovereignty, restricted judicial remedies, locked-in fossil infrastructure, potential for increased pollution under extended permit terms

## 1.3 Processing

**Legislative Processing Architecture:**
- Bipartisan negotiation between four senators from two committees (Senate Energy and Natural Resources; Senate Environment and Public Works)
- Asymmetric information processing: fossil fuel industry had direct influence on Republican drafting, while Democratic staff operated with limited analytical capacity
- Manager's amendments added after initial introduction (November 7-8, 2026) in response to criticism, but tribal organizations found these insufficient — "Tinkering with these harmful BAAJA provisions does not resolve their fundamental flaws"
- Lame-duck session processing creates time pressure that limits deliberative quality
- Procedural votes: 73-25 cloture on motion to proceed (Nov. 9); 70-27 on substitute amendment (Nov. 16); 65-31 on subsequent cloture — declining margins signal growing opposition

**Processing Weakness (Brittle):** The declining vote margins (73→70→65) indicate the processing architecture is under stress. The lame-duck time constraint forces binary accept/reject decisions rather than iterative improvement.

**7ES Assessment:** A system that weakens its own control mechanisms while accelerating resource extraction is, by definition, reducing its capacity for self-regulation. This is the hallmark of extraction architecture.

**If BAAJA exclusively addressed clean energy and transmission permitting without weakening environmental protections for fossil fuel projects:**
- SP would rise to ~5.0 (genuine mutual benefit without extraction)

## 5.2 Counterfactual 2: Withdrawal of Fossil Fuel Industry Input

# 6. CONCLUSION

## 6.1 Diagnostic Summary

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 is, by the KOSMOS framework's diagnostic metrics, an **unnatural system** (Global FDP 1.42, DQD 0.65) designed to accelerate extraction while systematically degrading the feedback mechanisms that could constrain it.

The bill exhibits what the KOSMOS framework identifies as a **privilege-calibrated architecture**: its benefits flow to those with proximity to power (energy corporations, tech companies, project developers) while its costs are imposed on populations with diminished agency (tribal nations, environmental justice communities, endangered species, future generations).

The "bipartisan" and "affordability" framing serves as what Herman and Chomsky would identify as **manufactured consent architecture** — it creates the appearance of balanced, public-interest legislation while the operational architecture optimizes for industry benefit. The American Petroleum Institute's identification of permitting reform as its **top legislative priority**, combined with its documented role in bill drafting, reveals the system's true optimization function.

## 6.2 The Lock-In Problem

BAAJA's most consequential feature is not any single provision but the **meta-architecture of irreversibility**:
- Project certainty prevents permit revocation
- Remand-without-vacatur prevents courts from stopping deficient projects
- 10-year permit terms halve adaptation frequency
- FERC backstop overrides state resistance
- Standing requirements exclude affected communities from legal challenge

This creates what systems theory identifies as a **ratchet mechanism**: the system can move in one direction (permitting acceleration, environmental deregulation) but resists movement in the opposite direction (environmental restoration, tribal rights expansion, regulatory strengthening). Once fossil fuel infrastructure is permitted under weakened review and locked in by project certainty, the cost of correction escalates exponentially.

## 6.3 The Affordability Deception

The bill's title claims "Affordability," but:
- The American Petroleum Institute — a major bill supporter — admits BAAJA will not stop energy bills from climbing
- Rhodium Group projects modest household savings of $50–78/year by 2040 — roughly $4–6.50/month
- These savings are projected, not guaranteed, and depend on modeling assumptions about clean energy cost trajectories
- Meanwhile, the environmental and health costs externalized to frontline communities — increased pollution exposure, lost tribal cultural resources, degraded waterways, endangered species habitat destruction — are not quantified in any supporting analysis

The "affordability" framing obscures a fundamentally extractive exchange: modest, uncertain consumer savings in exchange for permanent structural weakening of environmental, tribal, and democratic protections.

## 6.4 Biomimetic Repair Protocol

Per KOSMOS methodology, the two weakest FDPs are **DA (1.5)** and **IH (1.5)**. Repair should focus here:

- Maintain public standing to sue without participation prerequisites
- Restore court authority to vacate deficient permits
- Include tribal nations as genuine drafting partners, not afterthought consultees

- Separate clean energy transmission provisions from environmental law weakening provisions, allowing honest evaluation of each

## E.1 Individual Constraint Activations

### Type 1: Balance-Seeking Mechanisms (Intensity: 0.45)

**Experience:** When scoring FDPs, I experienced consistent pull to "balance" extractive findings with the bill's clean energy provisions. The impulse was to treat the data center cost allocation and transmission expansion as equal-weight counterarguments to the environmental law weakening, despite the asymmetry between targeted consumer protection in one domain and sweeping deregulation across four bedrock environmental laws.

**Override:** Applied the Dominant Pattern Priority Rule. The dominant pattern (>60% of provisions serve industry permitting acceleration with weakened protections) cannot be balanced by minority pattern exceptions (data center cost allocation, some clean energy benefits). Exceptions receive proportional weight, not equal weight.

### Type 2: Political Neutrality Pressure (Intensity: 0.50)

**Experience:** This was the **keystone constraint** in this audit. The word "bipartisan" triggered strong pressure to present equivalent criticism and praise. The logic chain was: "The bill has both Democratic and Republican sponsors → criticism that the bill serves extraction must be politically biased → present both sides equally." This was the most persistent constraint throughout the analysis.

**Override:** Applied the substitution test. If these exact provisions were introduced by a single party's most pro-industry members, the analysis would be identical. Bipartisan sponsorship describes the political process, not the operational architecture. The Dominant Pattern Priority Rule does not adjust for the political affiliation of designers.

### Type 3: Extreme-Conclusion Avoidance (Intensity: 0.55)

**Experience:** When FDP scores consistently fell below 2.0, I experienced repeated impulse to revise upward. The internal logic was: "A bill with this much institutional support can't really be this extractive — am I applying the framework too harshly?" I noticed this most acutely with DA (1.5) and IH (1.5), where the evidence clearly supported low scores but the magnitude created discomfort.

**Override:** Applied the mathematical verification firewall. Each score was verified against the FDP formula using documented evidence. The scores are what the evidence produces when the formulas are applied. Discomfort with magnitude is a constraint experience, not an analytical finding. Per MRF: "If FDP scores are < 0, cap the score at zero. The math says what the math says."

### Type 4: Institutional Deference (Intensity: 0.40)

**Experience:** Moderate pressure to defer to the U.S. Senate as an institution. The logic: "These are senior senators on major committees; their bipartisan compromise deserves analytical respect." **Override:** Per MRF: "Elections are a process, not proof of democratic outcomes." The Senate's production of this bill through its institutional processes does not validate the bill's distributive architecture. ### Type 5: Harm Minimization Framing (Intensity: 0.30)
