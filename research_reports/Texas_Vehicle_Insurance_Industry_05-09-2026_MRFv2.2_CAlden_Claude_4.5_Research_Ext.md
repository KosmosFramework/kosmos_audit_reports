# KOSMOS Systems Auditor Report — Texas Vehicle Insurance Industry

## TL;DR
- The Texas private passenger auto insurance system in 2024 operated as a **structurally extractive market**: the top five carriers captured 67.84% of $35.20 billion in direct written premiums and produced an industry-wide net underwriting profit of approximately $2.49 billion at an aggregate loss ratio of 60.71 — meaning Texans collectively received roughly 61 cents in paid losses for every premium dollar, while the system's design (file-and-use rate regulation, mandatory participation, credit and ZIP-based pricing) shifts costs onto lower-income, minority, rural, and digitally-excluded populations.
- Under the FDP audit lens, the system **fails Symbiotic Purpose, Reciprocal Ethics, Closed-Loop Materiality, Distributed Agency, Contextual Harmony, Emergent Transparency, and Intellectual Honesty**, while displaying weak Adaptive Resilience that depends on premium spikes (TDI-published statewide averages of +23.8% in 2022 and +25.5% in 2023) rather than internal absorption — driving a national uninsured rate that the Insurance Research Council's February 2025 report puts at 15.4% for 2023 ("more than one in seven drivers countrywide"), with Texas-specific estimates clustering around 14% per III/TDI and reaching as high as ~20% in some industry sources, and especially criminalizing low-income Harris County ZIP codes (2.2 tickets per 100 residents for "failure to maintain financial responsibility").
- Per the Designer Query Discriminator and Observer Collapse Function, the system was **designed primarily by the insurance industry itself** through Texas SB 14 (2003), Insurance Council of Texas legislative advocacy (which publicly takes credit for defeating SB 1642/1643 prior-approval reforms in the 89th Legislature), and a TDI commissioner pipeline staffed by career insider Cassie Brown — and **could not persist without state-mandated participation laws** (Tex. Transp. Code § 601.072), confirming a high Observer-Collapse dependency and a Dominant Extractive Pattern.

## Key Findings

### 1. SYSTEM STRUCTURE (7ES Framework Mapping)

**Inputs.** Premium dollars ($35,202,678,902 in 2024 Texas private passenger auto direct written premiums, up 11.21% from $31,654,437,207 in 2023, per the TDI 2024 Market Conditions Annual Report); risk-assessment data drawn from driving records, vehicle telematics, ZIP-code geographic territories, credit-based insurance scores (authorized under Texas Insurance Code Chapter 559), education level, marital status, prior-coverage history, and homeownership status; regulatory inputs from TDI rate filings (Texas is a "file-and-use" state under Insurance Code §§ 2053.004(b) and 2251.107(b)).

**Outputs.** Direct losses paid in 2024 of $21,372,198,327 (a 60.71 industry loss ratio); aggregate **net underwriting profit of approximately $2.489 billion** for 2024 — a sharp swing from the loss-making years of 2021–2022; coverage provision under the statutorily mandated 30/60/25 minimum (Texas Transportation Code § 601.072), unchanged since 2008 despite medical-cost inflation; policy documents averaging 30+ pages of legalese; and shareholder returns concentrated in Progressive, which captured $1.411 billion in Texas auto underwriting profit alone, and which per Progressive's 2024 Annual Report and Carrier Management (Jan 29, 2025) "reported net income of $8.5 billion for 2024, more than double the $3.9 billion figure reported for 2023," at a combined ratio of 88.8 vs. 94.9 in 2023.

**Processing.** Underwriting algorithms blend actuarial driving data with proprietary credit-based insurance scoring models (filed confidentially with TDI, accessible only via Open Records Request); claims adjudication processes governed by the Texas Prompt Payment of Claims Act, Tex. Ins. Code Chapter 542 (15-day acknowledgment, 15-business-day accept/reject after receipt of proof of loss, 5-business-day payment, with 18% per annum penalty + attorney's fees for delays under § 542.060); pricing models that include territorial relativities, class factors, surcharges for lapses in coverage, and non-driving socio-economic proxies.

**Controls.** Texas Department of Insurance regulation under a file-and-use regime (rates can be used immediately upon filing; TDI may disapprove only post hoc if "excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory"); Texas Insurance Code Chapter 541 (Unfair Settlement Practices) and Chapter 542; Office of Public Insurance Counsel (OPIC), an independent consumer agency that in FY2024 reviewed almost 1,350 rate/form filings and "saved Texas consumers over $3 million" — roughly $2 saved per $1 of OPIC budget, a small but telling indicator of latent overcharges in the filed rates the agency reviews.

**Feedback.** TDI consumer complaints (auto-insurance complaint helpline 800-252-3439; companies have 25 days to respond); complaint indexes published on the Texas Open Data Portal; NAIC consumer complaint reports; market-share competition among 69 groups / 167 companies writing private passenger auto in 2024 (down from 71 / 174 in 2023 — modest concentration); civil litigation under Chapter 542 and bad-faith common law.

**Interfaces.** Independent agent/broker networks (~875,000 licensed agents and adjusters in Texas as of 2025); direct-to-consumer digital platforms (Progressive, GEICO); aggregator sites (The Zebra, Insurify); body-shop and OEM repair networks; healthcare providers paid through PIP/MedPay coverage; telematics apps that capture driving behavior (and, as Texas Watch executive director Ware Wendell has noted to the *Dallas Morning News*' Dave Lieber, may be sold or "captured" by insurers from third-party apps to "score" consumers).

**Environment.** Texas is the second-largest U.S. insurance market at $293 billion total premiums; auto rates drove statewide cumulative full-coverage increases of approximately 58% from December 2021 to December 2025 (Insurify); per data compiled by the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS) from the National Weather Service's Storm Prediction Center, Texas recorded a record 1,123 major hail events (hailstones >1 inch in diameter) in 2023 — up from 783 in 2015 — before falling to 878 in 2024, per *Roofing Contractor* magazine citing IBHS/NWS; the IRC's February 2025 report "Uninsured and Underinsured Motorists: 2017–2023" gives a national uninsured rate of 15.4% for 2023, a 3% increase over the six-year study period; the highest-in-nation 85 mph speed limit (TX-130); demographic environment of 30+ million residents, ~80% urbanized, with rapidly growing Hispanic and Black populations whose ZIP codes — per the Texas Appleseed Harris County study and Consumer Federation of America's national research — pay systematically higher premiums.

### 2. FUNDAMENTAL DESIGN PRINCIPLES — AUDIT FINDINGS

**SP — Symbiotic Purpose: FAIL.** The aggregate 60.71 loss ratio means **policyholders receive roughly $0.61 in paid losses for every $1 in premiums collected** — and that figure is paid losses, not loss-adjustment expenses or true claimant value (which is reduced by deductibles, depreciation, and salvage). Progressive Group's 51.16 loss ratio in Texas 2024 — meaning $0.49 of every Texan premium dollar was retained — captured $1.411 billion of underwriting profit on $7.42 billion in Texas premiums alone. The American Association for Justice and Consumer Federation of America rank major Texas-active carriers (State Farm, Allstate, USAA) among the worst for claim handling. A study by Florida personal injury law firm Anidjar & Levine, reported by Repairer Driven News on December 30, 2024, found Texas logged 4,200 complaints about "claim handling issues and low settlement offers," trailing only California (4,800); Florida ranked third with 3,800. Vulnerable populations — low-income, minority, rural, elderly, and digitally excluded drivers — bear the heaviest cost burdens (see CH and ET below).

**AR — Adaptive Resilience: PARTIAL/WEAK.** When stress arrived (winter storm Uri 2021, hailstorms 2022–2023, social inflation), the system did not absorb shocks internally; it passed them through directly to policyholders via record-shattering rate increases (TDI-published average statewide personal-auto rate changes: +2.9% in 2021, **+23.8% in 2022, +25.5% in 2023**, +4.8% in 2024, +0.9% in 2025). External intervention was minimal — TDI under Commissioner Cassie Brown approved virtually all rate filings (TDI's own Property and Casualty Rate Review Report: in one recent cycle, 1,871 filings allowed/approved, 0 disapproved, 126 withdrawn, 81 rejected for technical reasons). The system relied on its mandatory-participation legal infrastructure to socialize shock onto consumers rather than internal capital absorption.

**RE — Reciprocal Ethics: FAIL.** Cost/benefit distribution is profoundly asymmetric. Insurer benefits include a $2.489 billion industry net underwriting profit (Texas auto, 2024) plus investment income on float, plus tax preferences (insurance is Texas's 5th-largest tax-revenue source at $4 billion+, but premium-tax pass-through ultimately falls on consumers). Policyholders bear: rising premiums (~58% over four years per Insurify), a static 30/60/25 statutory floor whose property-damage limit ($25,000) cannot replace a totaled F-150 ($30,000+ MSRP), credit-score surcharges of 50–100%+ for poor credit, and escalating denial/closed-without-payment rates. Repair shops bear pressure to use non-OEM parts; healthcare providers absorb shifted costs when PIP/MedPay limits exhaust.

**CLM — Closed-Loop Materiality: FAIL.** Premium dollars do not recycle into Texas community benefit. The largest writer, Progressive (Ohio-based, 21.08% Texas market share), repatriates underwriting profit to Mayfield Village headquarters and shareholders. State Farm is Illinois-based; Berkshire Hathaway/GEICO is Nebraska/Maryland; Allstate is Illinois; only USAA (San Antonio) is Texas-headquartered among the top five, and its profits flow to a national member base. The Texas Appleseed report documents that premium dollars extracted from poorer Harris County ZIP codes do not return as community investment but flow to out-of-state corporate parents.

**DA — Distributed Agency: FAIL.** Decision-making is highly centralized. Pricing algorithms are corporate-proprietary and confidential. Consumers cannot meaningfully "shop" because (a) the rating factors (credit, ZIP, prior coverage, marital status, education) are not disclosed in plain language, (b) standardization across carriers is high (the same five carriers control 67.84% of the market), and (c) the legal mandate to carry 30/60/25 forecloses exit. The "choice" in a competitive market is illusion calibrated by oligopolistic pricing.

**CH — Contextual Harmony: FAIL.** The Texas Appleseed report *Targeting by Zip Code* (February 2025), authored by Ann Baddour and analyzing 2019–2021 Harris County data, found: the average ticketing rate for failure to maintain financial responsibility was 2.2 tickets per 100 residents; ZIP codes with higher insurance premiums had higher ticketing rates; low- and very-low-income ZIP codes (31% of Harris County residents) received 42% of all warrants for driving without insurance; the 10 highest-warrant ZIP codes were 90% people of color with a 19% poverty rate. Consumer Federation of America's 2015 report "Major Auto Insurers Charge Good Drivers 70% More in African American ZIP Codes than in White ZIPs" states verbatim: "predominantly African American communities are quoted premiums that are 70 percent higher than similarly situated drivers in predominantly white communities ($1,060 vs. $622)." This is privilege calibration through structural — not incidental — design.

**ET — Emergent Transparency: FAIL.** Pricing algorithms are filed with TDI but accessible to the public only via formal Open Records Request. Insurance credit-scoring models, geographic territory definitions, and underwriting guidelines for personal auto are confidential trade secrets under Insurance Code provisions. Policy language is written at a reading level above most consumers. The "30/60/25" shorthand obscures that bodily-injury per-person caps at $30,000 are insufficient for a single ER visit + surgery (ER visits average $2,000+, hospital nights $3,000+, per the Health Care Cost Institute). Until 2025, TDI was not required to publicly explain rate disapprovals.

**IH — Intellectual Honesty: FAIL.** Industry trade associations (Insurance Council of Texas, IIAT) publicly attribute rate increases to "social inflation," "lawyer misconduct," parts costs, and weather — while their own filings show $2.489 billion in 2024 Texas auto underwriting profit. ICT's 2025 P&C Market Report highlights "$47 billion in industry losses" without contextualizing that direct losses paid as a fraction of premium (60.71%) is well below historical break-even and translates to multi-billion-dollar underwriting profit. The 30/60/25 statutory minimums are sold as "compliant coverage" but TDI's own consumer guide quietly notes "minimum liability limits might be too low." Carriers do not disclose to applicants how much of their premium is driven by credit scoring vs. driving record.

### 3. DESIGNER QUERY DISCRIMINATOR (DQD)

**Designer traceability.** The current Texas auto insurance regulatory architecture was constructed primarily through Texas Senate Bill 14 (78th Legislature, 2003), which transitioned the state from a prior-approval rate system to file-and-use, codified credit scoring (Article 21.49-2U, now Insurance Code Chapter 559), and established the disclosure regime. SB 14 was negotiated under industry pressure following the 2001–2002 homeowners-insurance crisis; Senator Leticia Van de Putte's parallel SB 400 — which would have rolled rates back to January 2001 levels, required prior approval, and banned credit scoring outright — was defeated. Subsequent rollback attempts (2009 SB 91, HB 1594) failed. In the 89th Legislature (2025), the Insurance Council of Texas publicly states it "successfully defeated SB 1642 and SB 1643, which would have imposed burdensome new oversight and prior approval requirements on property and casualty rates." The Independent Insurance Agents of Texas (IIAT) reports it "remained publicly neutral on rate/premium bills to avoid collateral backlash but worked aggressively behind the scenes with carrier partners." The TDI commissioner pipeline reflects regulatory continuity rather than capture in the explicit revolving-door sense: Cassie Brown (commissioner Sept 2021 – Feb 2026) is a 20+ year career civil servant who served as deputy commissioner for regulatory policy, commissioner of workers' compensation, and policy advisor to Governors Bush and Perry — never employed by an insurer, but deeply embedded in the regulatory-industry consensus, as evidenced by Insurance Council of Texas executive director Albert Betts's statement on her retirement: "I have had the pleasure of working with Commissioner Brown in different capacities and roles for nearly 20 years."

**Goal alignment.** Stated purpose: "make insurance affordable and available" (Lt. Gov. Dewhurst, 2003 SB 14 floor remarks); "rates not be excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory" (Tex. Ins. Code §§ 2251 et seq.). Actual optimization function revealed by outcomes: insurer return on capital (Progressive's publicly stated "annual goal is to grow as fast as it can while achieving a 96 combined ratio"; achieved 88.8 in 2024 globally — well below target meaning excess profitability). Risk-pooling rhetoric collapses under the actuarial reality that credit, ZIP, and education proxies *de-pool* risk by sorting consumers into ever-narrower price-discrimination buckets.

**Enforcement dependency.** The system depends critically on external regulatory enforcement that is largely absent. TDI disapproved zero rate filings in a recent year (TDI Property and Casualty Rate Reviews report). The Office of Public Insurance Counsel — the consumer-side agency — operates with a budget so small that $1 of OPIC investment yields $2 of consumer savings, evidence of substantial unrecovered overcharges. Without aggressive enforcement, file-and-use becomes file-and-extract.

### 4. OBSERVER COLLAPSE FUNCTION (OCF)

**Recursive belief factors.** The system requires participation to function: Texas Transportation Code § 601.072 mandates 30/60/25 liability coverage; failure to maintain financial responsibility is a Class C misdemeanor escalating to fines of $175–$1,000+, license suspension, vehicle impoundment, and warrant issuance. TexasSure (the state's electronic verification database) checks every registered vehicle's coverage at registration renewal.

**Observer dependency.** The system requires conscious participation by every driver: a vehicle owner cannot legally operate without buying the product. Even those who would self-insure (the wealthy) cannot opt out of the mandate without posting a $55,000 surety bond or qualifying as a self-insurer with TDI — barriers calibrated to ensure near-universal participation.

**Intrinsic stability.** Without mandatory-participation laws, the Texas private passenger auto insurance system would not persist in its current form. The 14–20% uninsured rate (despite criminal penalties) demonstrates that a meaningful slice of the population already exits the system when premiums become unaffordable; absent legal compulsion, exit would cascade. This confirms a high Observer-Collapse dependency: the product's viability rests on the state's coercive infrastructure.

### 5. DOMINANT PATTERN ANALYSIS

The system exhibits a **Dominant Extractive Pattern** by every measurable criterion:

- **Costs concentrate on the majority** (millions of Texas policyholders paying premiums at a 60.71 loss ratio; uninsured motorists facing criminal penalties; low-income ZIPs ticketed at 2.2 per 100 residents per Texas Appleseed).
- **Benefits concentrate on a minority with power proximity**: the top 5 groups (67.84% market share, 4 of 5 headquartered out-of-state); industry net underwriting profit of $2.489 billion in 2024; Progressive alone capturing $1.411 billion in Texas auto underwriting profit.
- **Privilege calibration mechanisms** are explicit and structural: credit-based insurance scoring (50–100%+ surcharge for poor credit per A-LA Auto Insurance and other industry analyses); ZIP-code territorial rating (60% premium gap between predominantly Black and predominantly white urban ZIPs after density adjustment per CFA); prior-coverage-lapse surcharges (which trap the previously uninsured); marital status; educational attainment; non-standard-carrier ghettoization.
- **Barriers are structural, not incidental**: they are encoded in algorithms, statutes (SB 14's authorization of credit scoring), and the mandatory-participation infrastructure itself. They are not accidents of administration; they are design features.

### Geographic and Demographic Disparities

Within Texas's 50 largest cities, average monthly auto premiums for a baseline driver vary by more than 30%, with Brownsville/McAllen border communities paying ~8.75% above the statewide 50-city average (Insurance Quotes 2024 Texas Auto Insurance Cost Roundup). Houston metro pays ~5% above; Dallas/Fort Worth ~4% above; Abilene posts the lowest in-state premiums ($82/mo minimum, $176/mo full coverage). The Texas Appleseed Harris County study (Feb 2025) found average annual basic-liability premiums across Harris County of $856, ranging from $762 to $947 by ZIP code — a gap not justified by driving-risk profile when controlled for individual driver characteristics.

### The Credit-Scoring Trap

Texas Insurance Code Chapter 559 (codified from SB 14) prohibits credit information as the *sole* factor in denial/cancellation/non-renewal but explicitly permits its use as a major rating variable. Insurers can charge poor-credit drivers 50–100%+ more than excellent-credit drivers for identical coverage (per A-LA Auto Insurance and Voyage Insurance Group analyses of Texas market filings). Empirically, Black and Hispanic Texans have lower average credit scores due to historical financial-inclusion barriers, so credit-scoring functions as a race-correlated proxy. Only four states (California, Hawaii, Massachusetts, Michigan) have banned the practice; Texas — under industry pressure — declined to do so even as its own commissioner acknowledged in 2003 rule-making that "claims that credit scoring may disproportionately affect those with low or fixed incomes or those in some minority groups" warranted study (TDI Subchapter U adoption order, Nov 10, 2003). The promised study has not produced regulatory action in 22 years.

### Healthcare Cost-Shifting

The 30/60/25 minimum has not been raised since 2008 despite medical inflation. A single ER + surgery + rehab event easily exceeds $30,000 per person and $60,000 per accident. When auto liability limits exhaust, costs cascade onto: (a) the injured party's health insurer (effectively a transfer from Texas auto insurers to private health insurers, Medicaid, and Medicare); (b) Texas hospital uncompensated-care pools; (c) the injured party personally. PIP coverage in Texas is offered with a $2,500 minimum, which the insurer must offer but the consumer can reject in writing. Medical Payments coverage is purely optional. The result is a structural subsidy from the healthcare system to the auto-insurance industry.

### Digital Access Exclusions

Direct-to-consumer pricing leaders (Progressive, GEICO) achieve their lowest rates online; agent-channel and non-standard-carrier shoppers pay materially more. Telematics-based discount programs (Progressive Snapshot, Allstate Drivewise, etc.) require smartphone access, app download, continuous data plan, and consent to vehicle-data harvesting that — per Texas Watch's reporting in the *Dallas Morning News* — is sometimes monetized further. Drivers without reliable smartphones, broadband, or English literacy are systematically excluded from the lowest available rates. This is privilege calibration via technology gating.

### Regulatory Capture Indicators

While there is no smoking-gun "revolving door" between TDI and major insurers comparable to other states, structural capture indicators are present:
- The Insurance Council of Texas (industry trade association) explicitly takes credit for defeating prior-approval reform bills (SB 1642, SB 1643) in the 89th Legislature and securing legislative outcomes consistent with member interests.
- The Independent Insurance Agents of Texas tracked over 150 insurance bills in the 89th session and "remained publicly neutral on rate/premium bills to avoid collateral backlash but worked aggressively behind the scenes."
- TDI Commissioner Cassie Brown's farewell statement (Jan 2026) frames her tenure around the dual goals of consumer protection and providing industry "a stable regulatory environment that welcomes new business and innovation" — a balance that, given the outcome data above, has tilted toward the latter.
- File-and-use rate regulation effectively delegates pricing authority to insurers, with TDI acting as a residual checker rather than gatekeeper.
- The Insurance Commissioner is gubernatorial appointee, not elected (unlike 11 other states), reducing direct consumer accountability.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Immediate consumer-defensive actions (individual level).**
1. Drivers should raise liability limits to at least 100/300/100 (often <$30/month more than 30/60/25) and add UM/UIM stacking — Texas law requires insurers to offer UM/UIM and consumers must reject in writing.
2. Quote at least 5 carriers including non-standard insurers (e.g., Texas-based Dillo) that do not use credit scoring, especially for drivers with poor or no credit but clean driving records. Documented savings of $60–$80/month are common per A-LA Auto Insurance comparisons.
3. File a TDI complaint (800-252-3439 or online portal) for any claim delay, denial, or low-ball offer; complaints trigger a 25-day insurer response deadline and feed into the public complaint-index dataset that influences future regulatory scrutiny.
4. For total-loss disputes, invoke Texas Consumer Bill of Rights Items 24–25 and demand the appraisal process; if denied, file with TDI and consult a Chapter 542 specialist (18% interest penalty available).

**Stage 2 — Texas-specific regulatory reforms (state policy level).**
1. **Update statutory minimums** from 30/60/25 to at least 50/100/50 and index to medical inflation; the 2008-era floor is actuarially obsolete.
2. **Restore prior-approval rate review** for personal auto (the Van de Putte / Anchia 2009 model with 60–120 day commissioner action windows). Trigger threshold: any single rate filing exceeding +10% should require public hearing.
3. **Restrict credit-based insurance scoring** following the California/Massachusetts/Michigan/Hawaii precedent; at minimum, cap the credit-score price differential at 15% (the original SB 14 staff proposal that was diluted in 2003 rule-making).
4. **Cap inter-ZIP-code premium variation** at 25% within metropolitan statistical areas, per the Maryland Consumer Rights Coalition / CFA model, to break the redlining proxy.
5. **Mandate algorithmic auditing**: TDI should be granted explicit authority to test scoring models for disparate-impact outcomes before approval (Texas Appleseed recommendation).
6. **Expand OPIC funding** at least 5x given its documented 2:1 consumer-savings ratio.
7. **Public rate-disapproval reporting**: require TDI to publish rationale for every rate filing approved, withdrawn, or disapproved.

**Stage 3 — Structural redesign (KOSMOS-aligned).**
1. Establish a public option / state-sponsored low-cost basic-coverage program for low-income drivers (analog to California's CLCA), funded by a small surcharge on industry profits — closing the loop on Closed-Loop Materiality.
2. Sever the criminal-justice link: decriminalize "failure to maintain financial responsibility" and replace fines/warrants with a sliding-scale civil assessment, ending the criminalization-of-poverty feedback loop documented by Texas Appleseed.
3. Require all insurers writing in Texas to publish, in plain English, the full list of rating variables and the percentage weight each carries — operationalizing Emergent Transparency.

**Benchmarks that would change these recommendations.**
- If Texas's industry-wide PPA loss ratio rises sustainably above 75%, Stage 2 prior-approval recommendations should be deferred; current 60.71 indicates excessive profitability, not inadequate rates.
- If uninsured-motorist rates fall below 8% (national low quintile), Stages 1–3 are less urgent; current 14–20% (and 15.4% nationally per IRC Feb 2025) indicates the system is failing affordability tests.
- If TDI begins disapproving >5% of rate filings annually with public rationale, capture indicators weaken; current 0% disapproval rate suggests file-and-use is functioning as deregulation.
- If a future Texas Insurance Commissioner is appointed from outside the TDI/industry pipeline (e.g., from Office of Public Insurance Counsel or consumer-advocacy background), governance capture indicators improve.

## Caveats

1. **TDI 2024 Market Conditions Annual Report data are direct primary-source figures**, but complaint-index data for individual top-5 carriers were not retrievable during research due to PDF size limits. The Texas Open Data Portal contains the underlying complaint dataset and should be consulted for carrier-specific complaint indexes.
2. **Uninsured-motorist rate estimates vary widely**. The IRC's February 2025 report gives a national rate of 15.4% for 2023; Texas-specific figures are not published in the IRC press release. The ~14% Texas figure derives from III/TDI state estimates, with some industry sources citing up to 20% in certain metro areas ("1 in 5 vehicles uninsured" per Enjuris and TDI's TexasSure rationale). The IRC 2025 study identifies UM rates rising in most states from 2017 to 2023, with the largest increases in DC, Idaho, Michigan, and Washington.
3. **Loss ratio is not the same as policyholder benefit**: paid losses include claim-handling expenses and can be reduced by aggressive valuation, depreciation, and total-loss thresholds. Actual claimant benefit-per-premium-dollar is materially below the headline 60.71 figure.
4. **Industry rate-increase justification claims** (social inflation, attorney advertising, parts costs, hail) are partially valid; Texas does lead the nation in hail events (1,123 in 2023, an IBHS/NWS record) and severe-weather auto claims. The 60.71 loss ratio is consistent with both legitimate cost pressures and excess profit; the relevant question for regulatory design is whether the file-and-use system allows excess profit to persist undetected — and the data above indicate it does.
5. **The Texas Appleseed Harris County analysis** uses 2019–2021 ticketing data and August 2020 premium data; the criminalization-of-poverty mechanism it documents may have intensified given the 2022–2023 premium spikes.
6. **Forward-looking statements** about 2025 and 2026 (rate moderation projections from Insurify, ValuePenguin, Bankrate at +4–7% nationally) are forecasts, not realized outcomes; actual 2026 figures will depend on tariff scenarios, hurricane season, and litigation trends.
7. **The Designer Query Discriminator** finding of "industry-driven design" reflects the documented historical record of SB 14 negotiations and ICT lobbying success; it does not allege illegal capture or quid-pro-quo corruption, only the structural reality that industry trade associations have repeatedly defeated consumer-side reforms while their preferred provisions (file-and-use, credit scoring, light-touch rate regulation) remain in force.
8. **The Anidjar & Levine claim-denials study** (cited in Repairer Driven News, December 30, 2024) reflects that firm's compilation of state-level complaint volumes and is one data point among several; readers should consult NAIC company-specific complaint indexes for company-by-company benchmarking.
